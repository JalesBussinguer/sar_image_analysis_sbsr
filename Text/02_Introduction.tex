\section{Introduction}

We have already lost about half of Cerrado native vegetation cover.
According to the Cerrado's land use and land cover (LULC)) map of 2025 produced by MapBiomas \cite{mapbiomas2026}, approximately \qty{49.9}{\percent} of its territory is covered with native vegetation, and other \qty{48}{\percent} is covered with different land use types, mainly related to agriculture and livestock.

However, monitoring the Cerrado native vegetation is challenging.
Its characteristic vegetation is a composition of a mosaic of different proportions of evergreen riparian forests, deciduous and semi-deciduous forests, savanna woodlands, shrublands and grasslands \cite{ribeirowalter2008}.
The biome also presents well-marked dry and wet seasons \cite{sano2021} which increases the complexity of the task for monitoring strategies such as Cerrado Real-Time Deforestation Detection System (DETER-Cerrado) that rely primarily on multispectral data and requires cloud-free scene conditions.

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) systems are well suited for mapping and monitoring changes in the vegetation cover since they are able to acquire data day-and-night regardless of cloud coverage or other climatological conditions on the scene.
These characteristics make SAR particularly valuable for systematic and continuous environmental monitoring systems.
Previous studies have used polarimetric SAR data to map Cerrado vegetation \cite{sano2021}, investigated the seasonal behavior of vegetation indices over preserved areas \cite{bussinguer2024} and proposed approaches for monitoring deforestation on the biome \cite{bottani2026}.

These studies demonstrated the relevance of SAR for Cerrado monitoring, but they did not address a related, distributional question: whether spatially separated areas assigned to the same vegetation class can be represented by a common statistical model.
This distinction matters because a thematic label does not, by itself, guarantee that the associated backscatter is statistically indistinguishable across space.

Statistical models provide a framework for characterizing the signal’s behavior, rather than relying on derived attributes or classification outcomes.
Such models support comparisons among spatial samples and help determine whether observed differences are significant or not.
In particular, stochastic-distance methods provide a formal basis for testing hypotheses about speckled data distributions and support despeckling, segmentation, and classification methods \cite{nascimento2010}.

Given this context, this paper focuses on the following scientific question: 
\begin{quote}
Can spatially separated samples of the same class be modeled by a common probability distribution of SAR intensity with the same parameters?
\end{quote}
We investigate this question using NISAR L-band and Sentinel-1 C-band intensity data for the Forest, Savanna, and Grassland formations.
For each formation, we test the null hypothesis that the samples share the same distribution.
We assess this hypothesis through distribution fitting and statistical comparisons of spatially distributed samples of the same class.
